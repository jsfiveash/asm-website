ASM's 2026 William B. Marye Award

Dr. Tim Horsley


The use of geophysical survey technologies not only to “see through the ground,” but to so do well and with confidence in what we see, has occurred on archeological sites around the world for decades. It remains underused. Why? Ground-penetrating radar, magnetometry, magnetic susceptibility, and other geophysical technologies remain expensive, although costs have declined. There are few university programs in the USA and abroad that offer training. But most critical? The dearth of skilled, highly experienced practitioners. Tim Horsley long has been among that small, and now growing number of magicians who produce maps of successive Native American villages in a floodplain, 17th-century European fortified outposts, Colonial-era plantation sites, and innumerable cemeteries entombing the ancestors.

Tim has invested in the latest technologies and in the exploration of their uses to better understand the earth in which much of our heritage is bound, awaiting release at the end of a shovel and point of a trowel. More importantly, Tim has invested in the Maryland archeological community, traveling from his home in Northern Illinois to the state, typically piggy-backing two or more projects to meet demand while minimizing costs. He shows up on site in his over-stuffed electric vehicle with its solar array charging batteries and his GPS-tracked array of gadgets which he pushes or pulls, often by himself, while archeologists stand by, moving grids and the odd branch obstructing Tim’s transects. No earth dug and little dust raised; but in the end, detailed, georeferenced maps of features identified by two or more methods, each with geophysical attributes that aid in their description and identification.

We don’t “ground-truth” Tim’s results when we excavate: we provide additional information and test the hypotheses he offers based on his rigorous analyses of the geophysical data. The results: the first successful identification and delineation of the 1634 fort at St. Mary’s City; superimposed Late Woodland villages at the Barton site near Cumberland; the first successful identification and mapping of the streets and buildings of Colonial Port Tobacco near La Plata; and maps of Colonial-era sites like Notley Hall and Smith’s St. Leonard, and many other plantation and cemetery sites.

Tim Horsley not only has made archeology easier and cheaper in Maryland: he has made it more rewarding, and he has done so in as collegial and unassuming a manner as one can hope to find among the top practitioners of any field. Thank you, Tim.
